## Answer

**Target:** Skyward, Inc. (`skyward.com`) is a long-running, privately held K-12 administration software company based in Stevens Point, Wisconsin. It is not a venture-style edtech startup; it looks like a durable, founder/family-owned vertical software business.

**Business model & product**

Skyward sells mission-critical administrative software to K-12 school districts, with two core product suites:

- **Student Information System:** enrollment/registration, scheduling, attendance, gradebook, report cards/transcripts, curriculum management, family access, messaging, food service, health services, behavior, college/career readiness, MTSS/interventions.
- **School ERP / business office suite:** HR, applicant tracking, benefits, position budgeting, financial management, purchasing, budgeting, AP/AR, general ledger, payroll/timeclock, employee portal, asset/warehouse management.

Commercial motion appears to be enterprise district sales, demos, school pricing requests, implementation, training/support, and add-on modules. Skyward also has a partner/integration ecosystem and cloud/hosting story via ISCorp; newer product expansion includes **DistrictPulse**, an analytics product for Qmlativ built with AWS QuickSight.

**Vertical niche & market position**

Skyward is squarely in **K-12 SIS + school ERP**, a niche where switching costs are high because systems touch grades, attendance, parent portals, payroll, finance, state reporting, integrations, and compliance. Skyward says it serves **2,700 customers, 7.8M students, and 23 states**. That makes it one of the larger U.S. K-12 administrative software vendors, though likely behind PowerSchool nationally.

Market position: credible incumbent, especially in districts that want an integrated SIS + ERP stack and strong support. Its long-term state/cooperative relationships are notable, including Washington's WSIPC ecosystem and prior state-preferred vendor wins.

**Estimated revenue / ARR & profitability signals**

Skyward is private, so audited revenue/EBITDA are not public. Public estimates are noisy:

- Third-party databases commonly estimate revenue around **$90M-$100M**; Owler's public snippet says **$95.5M**.
- Latka's public snippet lists **$45.1M ARR in 2020**, but this should be treated cautiously because private-company SaaS databases can conflate sources and are not audited.
- With ~700 employees, the implied revenue per employee at a $95M estimate is roughly **$135K**, which is not obviously high for mature services-heavy K-12 software. If revenue is closer to $45M ARR, profitability would depend heavily on services/support load.
- Profitability signal is more qualitative: Skyward has operated since 1980, built a large headquarters, has no obvious dependency on VC/PE financing, and presents as a family-owned, internally built company. That points to a disciplined, likely self-sustaining business, but EBITDA cannot be confirmed publicly.

**Employee count & growth trend**

Employee signals cluster around **650-740**:

- LinkedIn lists **501-1,000 employees** and shows ~680 associated employees.
- Skyward's own 2020 CEO profile said the company had "nearly 700" employees.
- Revelio Labs estimates **740 employees as of March 2026**, up from **695 in 2023**, implying modest growth and a fairly stable workforce, not hypergrowth.

**Ownership & funding history**

Skyward appears to be **founder/family-owned and bootstrapped or at least not institutionally funded**.

- Founded in 1980 by **Jim and Jean King**.
- In 2020, founder Jim King named his son **Jerry King** chairman and stated the family was committed to keeping Skyward in the family.
- Skyward's own about page emphasizes a "family-owned legacy" and "building solutions, not buying them."
- No public, source-verifiable VC/PE round history or named outside investors surfaced. Some third-party databases show ~$8.1M-$8.2M "funding," but without public round detail or named investors; I would not underwrite an acquisition thesis on that without confirming directly.

**Key competitors**

Primary competitors in K-12 SIS / school admin software:

- **PowerSchool**
- **Infinite Campus**
- **Edupoint Synergy**
- **Tyler SIS / Tyler Technologies school products**
- **Focus School Software**
- **Aeries**
- **Frontline Education**, especially for adjacent HR/admin/workforce modules
- Smaller/private-school or lighter-weight SIS players: Gradelink, Alma, FACTS/RenWeb, Blackbaud, Jotform-linked/lightweight tools

**Moat / defensibility**

Skyward's moat is classic vertical software:

- **High switching costs:** SIS/ERP replacement is painful, multi-year, and touches every district stakeholder.
- **Regulatory/state reporting complexity:** K-12 state and federal reporting requirements create local adaptation and support needs.
- **Installed base:** 2,700 customers and 7.8M students is a large embedded footprint.
- **Integrated suite:** Combining SIS, finance, HR, payroll, parent/student access, reporting, and services reduces vendor sprawl.
- **Support reputation:** Skyward explicitly positions support as what sets it apart.
- **Family/private ownership:** May enable patient roadmap execution and customer trust, though it can also slow capital-intensive modernization.

**Risks**

- **Modernization / migration risk:** Skyward is moving customers from SMS 2.0 to Qmlativ. Migrations are operationally risky, and public district pages show many districts preparing 2025-2026 cutovers.
- **PowerSchool scale pressure:** PowerSchool and other larger platforms can outspend on product, integrations, AI, compliance, and M&A.
- **UI/user sentiment risk:** Public reviews and K-12 tech forums often criticize legacy SIS products, including Skyward, for usability or performance.
- **Cyber/data sensitivity:** SIS/ERP platforms hold student, family, payroll, health, and financial data. Skyward's Trust Center addresses security/privacy, but the category itself is high-risk.
- **Procurement concentration:** K-12 sales cycles are slow, budget-sensitive, and influenced by state/cooperative contracts.
- **Family ownership deal risk:** If the family is serious about generational ownership, founder outreach should be patient and values-led, not purely financial-engineering led.

**Recent news / signals, last 12-18 months**

- **Feb/Mar 2025:** Skyward announced **DistrictPulse**, a district analytics product developed with AWS, positioned as the biggest product announcement since Qmlativ.
- **2025-2026:** Multiple districts publicly discussed or executed **Qmlativ migrations**, suggesting a meaningful installed-base transition cycle.
- **2024 award still relevant into the period:** Qmlativ won the **2024 Wisconsin Innovation Award** software category, giving the modernization effort external validation.
- **2026 hiring/activity:** LinkedIn posts and Revelio data indicate continued hiring, though not explosive growth.

**Personalization hooks for founder/family outreach**

Best outreach angle: respect the company as a **45-year, Wisconsin-rooted, family-owned K-12 software institution** rather than pitching it like a distressed or growth-at-all-costs startup.

Useful hooks:

- "Skyward's journey from the King family's Stevens Point garage to 2,700 customers and 7.8M students is exactly the kind of founder-led vertical software story we admire."
- Reference **Jim and Jean King**, **Jerry King's chairmanship**, and the public commitment to keeping Skyward family-led.
- Mention **Scott Glinski's intern-to-CEO path** as a culture signal; he started as employee #17 and became CEO in 2019.
- Tie thesis to **preserving support culture**, helping fund Qmlativ/DistrictPulse modernization, and protecting district trust.

Acquisition read: **attractive but likely hard to buy.** This is a high-retention, mission-critical vertical software company with a meaningful customer base and durable niche. The biggest question is not whether the business is valuable; it is whether ownership has any willingness to transact, and whether a buyer can credibly preserve the family/customer-support identity that appears central to Skyward's brand.
