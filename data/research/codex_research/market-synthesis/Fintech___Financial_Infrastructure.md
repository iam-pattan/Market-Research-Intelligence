## Answer

**Segment definition:** I'd define "Fintech & Financial Infrastructure" B2B vertical software as software and API platforms sold into financial institutions, fintechs, merchants, lenders, wealth managers, insurers, payment facilitators, and finance teams: core/digital banking, issuer/acquirer processing, vertical payments, risk/fraud/AML/KYC, lending workflows, AP/AR automation, wealth/advisor tech, capital-markets ops, reconciliation, reporting, and compliance.

**TAM and growth:** The addressable software pool is large and still growing faster than GDP. Broad financial-services software is estimated around **$160B-$180B globally in 2025-2026**, growing roughly **8%-12% CAGR** depending on definition. Narrower infrastructure pockets are smaller but faster: core banking software is commonly sized around **$13B-$20B in 2025** with **10%+ CAGR**, while embedded-finance/FaaS and payment-infrastructure definitions can produce much larger figures because they include platform/service economics, not just software license revenue. For underwriting a founder-owned vertical-software asset, I'd use a bottom-up TAM by workflow, customer count, ARPA, attach-rate, and payments/revenue-share opportunity, not a broad "fintech" headline.

**Fragmentation vs. consolidation:** This is a **barbell market**. The infrastructure layers with scale/network effects are consolidating: merchant acquiring, issuer processing, fraud networks, wealth platforms, and capital-markets/regulatory data. KPMG's H1 2026 Pulse of Fintech shows capital concentrating into fewer, larger, proven platforms, especially payments. But the application layer remains highly fragmented because regulations, integrations, customer workflows, and vertical use cases differ by geography and end market. SEG's 2Q26 SaaS report says vertically focused software represented **54% of SaaS M&A activity**, and financial services was the second-largest vertical category at **12.8%** of vertical SaaS deals.

For SMB fintech vertical software, premium valuation usually requires retention, compliance depth, clean gross margins, and real payments/data monetization.

**Underserved niches / whitespace:**

- **Vertical B2B payments** in complex workflows: healthcare, legal trust accounting, construction, insurance premium finance, property management, dealerships, government fees, freight/logistics, travel, fitness/wellness, and field services.
- **Community bank and credit union modernization:** core-adjacent tools, digital onboarding, loan origination/servicing, exception handling, compliance automation, statement/data migration, and vendor-risk workflows.
- **Sponsor-bank and BaaS governance:** program monitoring, ledger controls, KYC/KYB, transaction monitoring, reconciliation, and regulatory evidence management.
- **Real-time payments and stablecoin treasury ops:** compliance-aware payment routing, settlement, reconciliation, FX, proof-of-reserves, and enterprise controls.
- **Wealth/RIA back office:** billing, advisor transitions, alternatives/private-credit workflows, model governance, client reporting, and compliance archiving.
- **AI-enabled fraud/risk ops:** mule detection, chargebacks, merchant underwriting, sanctions screening, document fraud, and human-in-the-loop investigation queues.
- **Legacy-to-cloud replacements** where incumbents are sticky but unloved: niche loan servicing, municipal/utility billing-payments, fund admin, tax forms, investment accounting, and regulatory reporting.

**What makes a durable, profitable, founder-owned niche leader:**

A great asset owns a painful, recurring, regulated workflow where failure is expensive. It has deep integrations into cores, ERPs, payment processors, bank rails, custodians, accounting systems, regulators, or merchant systems; high gross retention; low logo churn; limited customer concentration; and support credibility that a generic AI or horizontal SaaS tool cannot easily replicate. The best ones are "system of record plus transaction layer": workflow software first, with embedded payments, compliance, data, or financing economics attached.

The founder-owned winners also tend to have disciplined economics: **70%+ gross margin**, efficient implementation, low CAC via referrals or channel partners, **20%+ EBITDA potential**, clean ARR/usage revenue definitions, strong security/compliance posture, and a narrow ICP where they can win repeatedly. AI helps when it is embedded into proprietary workflow/data loops; it hurts valuation when the product is just a wrapper around generic automation.
